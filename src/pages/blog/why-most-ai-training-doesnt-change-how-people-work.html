How we teach

Why most AI training doesn't change how people work

edBuddy Pro team7 October 20266 min read

Ask a room of professionals whether they've "done some AI training" and many hands go up. Ask how many use AI every day for real work, and far fewer stay up. The gap isn't about intelligence or interest. It's about how most AI training is built.

The demo trap

A lot of AI training is a series of impressive demonstrations. Someone generates a marketing plan in thirty seconds, summarises a 50-page report, writes a poem about the quarterly results. The audience is impressed. Then they go back to their desk, open an empty chat window, and don't know what to type for the task in front of them.

Watching someone else use a tool is a different skill from using it yourself, on your own work, with your own constraints. A demo shows what's possible. It doesn't build the habit.

Generic content, specific jobs

An HR generalist, a finance controller and a sales manager have very different weeks. One is screening CVs and handling policy questions; one is closing the books and explaining variances; one is researching accounts and following up after calls. A course that teaches "AI for everyone" with the same examples leaves each of them to translate it into their own work alone. Most don't.

That's why we build courses by role. The examples, the labs and the governance questions are the ones you'll actually meet on Monday morning.

What actually changes behaviour

1. Practise on real artefacts

The fastest way to make a habit stick is to use the tool on a task you'd be doing anyway. In our HR course, every module ends in a lab built on a real HR document: a job description, a screening rubric, an onboarding plan, a review. You leave with work you can use, not just notes.

2. Learn the why, not just the prompt

Prompt lists are useful for getting started, but they break the moment your task is a little different. Understanding why a prompt works (role, task, context, format, limits) lets you write your own for anything.

3. Know where to stop

People hesitate to use AI when they aren't sure what's allowed. Clear guidance on data privacy, bias and which decisions must stay with a person builds the confidence to use it properly, rather than nervously or not at all.

4. Small, frequent wins

Short lessons with a lab you can finish in ten minutes fit a working week. One saved hour on a real task does more for adoption than a full day's workshop that's forgotten by Friday.

A simple test for any AI course

Will you produce something you'd use at work by the end of the first lesson? If not, it's probably a demo, not training.

For teams

If you're bringing AI into a team, start with one workflow everyone shares, agree what data can and can't go in, and give people time to practise on real work. Adoption follows usefulness, not mandates.
